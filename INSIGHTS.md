## Likely range, and whether it beat two simple paths

For up to 24 of the tightest storages with at least 400 days of readings, a small open time-series foundation model (Chronos-Bolt small, Apache-2.0, CPU) produced a 10th-to-90th percentile band for the next 64 days. That horizon stays inside the length the model was trained to predict and covers Christmas through late January. The dry, typical and wet lines on the page are not from this model. They replay summers.

The last 60 days were held out. Error is the average miss, in megalitres, of the middle of the band. The same-date-last-year path uses the volume 365 days earlier. The last-month path carries forward the average daily change of the 30 days before the hold-out.

Series scored: 24. Average miss, middle of the band: 35,340 ML (3.4% of that storage’s capacity). Same-date-last-year: 187,798 ML (19.3% of capacity). Last-month pace: 80,127 ML (6.5% of capacity). The band’s middle beat the same-date-last-year path on 22 of 24 storages, and beat the last-month pace on 17 of 24.

## Rain and evaporation cross-check

For each tracked storage, daily change was lined up with that day’s rain and reference evaporation. The fit is kept only when rain has a positive link and explains at least 5% of the day-to-day change. Releases and demand dominate many dams, so a weak fit is discarded rather than used for the briefing.

Usable fits: 28 of 63 tracked storages. On those, the dry-weather pace and the driest-summer pace disagree about crossing 40% before Christmas in 5 cases. The page uses the driest-summer pace, because it is taken straight from the storage record.

## How the paths are defined

- Accessible capacity, latest volume and percent full come from the Bureau’s water storage dashboard.
- Daily history starts on 1 January 2022. A gap between readings is spread evenly across the missing days.
- A completed summer is 1 November to 31 March. The four summers from 2022–23 to 2025–26 are the analogues. The current summer is not used as an analogue.
- A summer counts only if it has at least 40 days of change.
- Dry path: the summer with the lowest average daily change. Typical: the middle summer. Wet: the highest.
- Last month: the average daily change over the 30 days up to the latest reading.
- Days of buffer: megalitres above 40% of capacity, divided by the daily fall. If the storage is not falling by at least 1 ML a day, the buffer is reported as holding through summer.
- Already under the line: official percent full below 40. Critical on the page’s colour scale is below 25.
- Watch: 40–60%, or still above 40% but the dry path crosses 40% before 31 March 2027.
- The 15% household cut is an illustration, not a measured saving from a past restriction.

## Streaming check and the batch rebuild

The same 30-day and 90-day change, the status band, and the days to the 40% line on the last-month pace are expressed as streaming queries and as a full batch rebuild. The measured times are in the benchmark section of the README once that job has run. Both use the snapshot, not a live feed.

## What this does not say

- It does not set a restriction, a pumping schedule, or an allocation.
- Power-station lakes are tracked but kept out of the “already under 40%” count on the front page, because a low hydro lake is not a town running out of drinking water.
- Town and farming roles are name rules (Warragamba is a town supply, Hume is a farming supply). They are a labelling choice so the levers are not asked to refill the wrong dam.
- 17 tracked supplies did not match a Bureau station coordinate and are left off the map.
- Where two states publish the same storage name, both rows are kept. Hume appears on the border.
- One national clock (Australian Eastern Daylight Time, UTC+11) is used for dates and weather. A reading in Western Australia can sit a few hours off that clock.
